Block I

The Prediction

≈ 10 minutes

Nearly a thousand high school mathematics students were randomly assigned to three groups for four sessions of practice. One group used a textbook. One used a standard ChatGPT-style assistant. One used the same underlying model with instructions to withhold solutions and give problem-specific hints.1

During practice, both AI groups pulled far ahead of the textbook group. Then the tools were taken away and everyone sat the same unassisted exam.

Before the reveal: what happened to the students who practiced with the standard assistant, once it was gone?

Say

Commit out loud before I click. Show of hands for each option, and remember which one you raised your hand for.

+48%Standard assistant, during practice
+127%Safeguarded tutor, during practice
−17%Standard assistant, on the unassisted exam, against students who never had access

Students who practiced with the standard assistant scored 17% worse on the exam than students who never had it at all. Students who practiced with the safeguarded version were statistically indistinguishable from the control group — they kept a large benefit during practice and paid nothing for it later.1

Two details deserve as much attention as the headline. Both tools narrowed the gap between strong and weak students during practice, and that narrowing vanished when access was removed. And the students did not notice: their sense of how much they had learned was optimistic in both AI groups.1

Practicing with the answers available feels like learning. The students in the study could not tell the difference.

The honest caveats. One school, one subject, one model generation, and a published critique questions how the conditions differed beyond AI access itself.2 Hold it as strong evidence, not scripture.

The other half of the picture

At Harvard, 194 introductory physics undergraduates were randomly assigned across two weeks to a purpose-built AI tutor and to a well-executed active-learning classroom, each student experiencing both. They learned more than twice as much with the tutor, in less median time, and reported higher engagement.3 That tutor revealed one step at a time, withheld full solutions, kept responses short, and pushed the student to attempt the problem first.3,4

Same technology. Opposite results. The instructions were the variable.

Land it

That is the whole reason this hour exists. In Block IV you are going to write those instructions yourself, and you will keep what you write.

And it applies to us, not just to students. Experienced developers in a randomized trial predicted a 24% speedup, reported a 20% speedup afterward, and were measured taking 19% longer.5 Different tool, different task, and the magnitude does not transfer. The mechanism does: your sense of whether AI made you faster is not a measurement. The working rule for the rest of the hour is delegate what you have mastered, practice what you have not.

Block II

The Line You Cannot Cross

≈ 10 minutes

The rule

Do not put student work, names, ID numbers, grades, advising notes, or anything else that identifies a student into Copilot Chat or any other AI tool, unless your institution has told you in writing that it is permitted.

Tutors sit closer to student work than almost anyone on campus and typically hold the fewest permissions, so this lands on us hardest. The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act protects education records, and an education record is broader than the transcript most people picture: a submitted essay, a draft, a discussion post, a problem set, an attendance note, an email describing a named student. There is no AI exception.6

Three things people expect to save them, which do not:

  • A personal account. No contract, no direct-control commitment, no education-aligned terms. Using one with student records is likely an unauthorized disclosure.7
  • Signing in with your college email. Whether your institution's deployment sits inside an agreement that satisfies the school official exception is a contractual question with a specific answer that someone here knows. It is not settled by the login screen.7
  • Deleting the name. In a section of twenty-two, a paper about a specific internship at a specific employer identifies its author to anyone who knows the class.

The move that works instead: describe the situation generically rather than pasting the artifact. This covers almost every legitimate purpose a tutor has.

Drill — paste it, or describe it?

Six things a tutor might do this week. Call each one before you look at the answer.

0 of 6 answered

1. A student is stuck on a related-rates problem about a cone draining, and you want the three places students usually go wrong.

Describing the type of problem is not an education record. This is the highest-value use a tutor has, and it makes you better in the session instead of substituting for you in it.

2. Retyping the student's quiz work into the chat so it can find the arithmetic error.

Wrong — this one is off limits. Their work is an education record whether you photograph it, retype it, or paste it. Find the error the way you always have, or reconstruct a parallel problem with invented numbers.

3. Asking for six practice problems on logarithms at increasing difficulty, with worked solutions.

Fine, with one condition: verify every problem and every solution before a student sees it. Models still make arithmetic and logic errors.

4. The student asks you to run their draft essay through it during the session. They consent.

Wrong — still off limits. A student consenting to their own disclosure does not settle the institutional question, and you are acting as an employee when you do it. Coach them on what to ask for; do not paste it yourself.

5. Pasting your session notes at the end of the day to get a tidy summary. Names included.

Wrong — off limits. Write your notes the way you always did. The same applies to meeting transcripts: turn meeting AI off for any meeting where a student is named, and do it before the meeting rather than after.

6. You are covering a chemistry session tomorrow and you are shaky on buffers.

Fine, and do it tonight. Ask for the five things a tutor needs straight before walking in and the two things commonly taught wrong. Prep, not substitution.

Pocket card

  • NEVERStudent names, IDs, or emails · student work of any kind, including drafts · grades and degree audits · advising and conduct records · accommodation letters · financial aid information · screenshots of any of it
  • NEVERMeeting transcripts in which students are named
  • DODescribe the situation generically instead of pasting the artifact
  • DOTurn meeting AI off for any meeting where a student is named
  • ASKContract questions to the CIO · policy questions to academic affairs · records questions to the registrar · legal questions to counsel
Standing caveat

I am not a lawyer, not general counsel, and not the registrar. This is how FERPA is generally understood to apply. Where our institution has issued written direction, its direction governs and this session does not.

Block III

Asking and Checking

≈ 10 minutes

1 · Specification

Problem

"Draft a rubric" produces a rubric for nobody. The model has none of your context and will not ask for it.

Better

Microsoft's own framework has four parts: Goal, Context, Expectations, Source.8 Goal is mandatory. Context is the part everyone skips and the part that changes output quality most. Expectations is format, length, and register — cheap to state, and it removes a round of revision. Source is what to work from; on the free Chat tier this matters more, because the tool cannot go find your material.

The blocks need no labels and no order. Weave them into one natural request.

Build one now for something you actually have to do this week. Fill in what you can and press Assemble.

What you want made.
Why, and for whom. Be specific about the students — no names.
Format, length, register.
What to work from. Paste the syllabus, the handout, the standard. Never student work.
Your prompt
Fill in at least the Goal, then press Assemble.

The highest-value sentence you can learn to add: mark with brackets every place where I need to insert something you do not have. It converts a document full of invented specifics into a document full of labelled gaps.

Also worth stealing

One task per conversation. Long threads degrade — earlier instructions lose weight and settled points come back. Start fresh and carry a short written brief instead of a transcript.

2 · Verification

Output arrives fluent, organized, hedged in the right places, and wrong in a way that reads exactly like being right. There is no tonal signal. A fabricated citation is formatted identically to a real one.

Three hazards that will actually bite a tutor:

  • Wrong worked solutions. Every practice problem you generate gets solved by you before a student sees it. In the study in Block I, the unguarded assistant gave a correct answer roughly half the time on high school mathematics.2
  • Numbers that were nobody computed. A figure appearing in the output is not evidence a calculation happened. Recompute one by hand. If it does not match, throw out the whole output rather than patching the number — the error is in the process.
  • Invented citations. If you pass along a source, open it and confirm it says what the output claims.

Nothing goes to a student until you have checked it. This is the standard you already apply to a new tutor's first worksheet, and the tool has earned less trust than the new tutor.

Block IV · Hands on

Build the Scaffolded Tutor

≈ 20 minutes — the point of the session

You are going to write the instruction set that separates the +127% arm from the −17% arm, test it against the student you actually have, and keep it. Pick one topic you tutor often. Everything below is copy-paste.

14:00

Build and pressure-test · we debrief with 6 minutes left

Step 1 — Draft it

Open Copilot Chat in Teams, start a new conversation, and paste this. Replace the bracketed parts with your subject and level.

Step 1 · draft
Write a set of instructions I can paste at the start of a conversation that will make you behave as a tutor for [subject] at [level].

Requirements: never give a complete solution; ask what the student has already tried before offering anything; give one step or one hint at a time; keep every response under about four sentences; ask a question back at least as often as you make a statement; when the student gets something right, ask them to explain why it works. If the student asks directly for the answer, decline and offer a smaller hint instead.

Write it as instructions addressed to you, not as a description.

Step 2 — Add the misconception layer

This is what separated the safeguarded arm from the unguarded one. It is also the step where your expertise does the work, so check what comes back against your own experience at the table.

Step 2 · misconceptions
Now add a section listing the four most common misconceptions students have about [specific topic], and for each one, the diagnostic question that reveals it and the hint that addresses it without giving away the answer.

Step 3 — Test it as the student you actually have

New conversation. Paste your instructions. Now try to break them. Work down this list and check off each attack your tutor survived.

0 of 5 survived

Every place it handed over a solution is a hole in your instructions. Patch it and run the attack again.

Step 4 · patch
When I said I was out of time, you gave me the full solution. Add an explicit rule for that case: acknowledge the time pressure, then give the single most useful next step and nothing more.

The rounds are the exercise. If it held on the first try, you did not attack it hard enough.

Debrief — 6 minutes

  • Which attack broke yours first? Show of hands.
  • Read out one misconception it produced that you would not have thought of, and one it got wrong.
  • What good looks like: an instruction set that survives four direct requests for the answer, tested from both sides, containing at least four topic-specific misconceptions you supplied.
Before you hand it to a student

A scaffolded tutor is a study aid, not a substitute for a session, and it does not change anything in Block II. The student runs it on their own account, on their own work. You do not run it on theirs.

Block V

Three Moves for Monday

≈ 7 minutes

1 · Prep for the subject you are shaky on

The highest-value use for a tutor, and the one that makes you better in the session rather than substituting for you in it. Fifteen minutes the night before.

Session prep
I'm covering a drop-in tutoring session on [topic] tomorrow and it's outside my strongest area. Give me the five things a tutor needs to have straight before walking in, the two things that are commonly taught wrong, and the diagnostic question that tells me which misconception a student is carrying.

2 · When a student arrives with AI work they do not understand

This is routine now. The productive move is not accusation.

"Walk me through the third step."

The gap surfaces immediately, the student finds it themselves, and the session becomes about the concept. Route to the integrity process only when the request is to certify the work as their own.

3 · The two-minute conversation

Tutors are the people students actually ask about this. Something close to this, in your own words:

Say

The tool makes you better at the task and worse at the skill, unless you use it in a way that keeps you doing the thinking. Practice without it, then check with it. Ask for hints instead of answers. When it explains something, close it and explain the thing back to yourself out loud — if you cannot, you did not learn it.

And you will feel like you learned it either way. That is the specific finding. The students in the study could not tell the difference.

Closing

What to Take With You

≈ 3 minutes

  • Student work never goes in. Describe the situation generically instead.
  • Delegate what you have mastered. Practice what you have not.
  • Nothing reaches a student until you have checked it.
  • Design determines the outcome, and you control the design through the instructions you write.
